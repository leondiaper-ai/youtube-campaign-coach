CorrectionHypothesis

The "22 of 23 channels had no follow-up" figure is wrong — and it's worth knowing before someone quotes it.

We went looking for the source. 22 of 23 is real, but it measures Shorts adoption in a curated 23-campaign set, not follow-up. The follow-up gap is a genuine pattern in individual deep dives; it has never been measured across the roster.

22 of 23 (95.7%) = campaigns including at least one Short
0 of 4 CHVRCHES heroes had a 7–14 day follow-up
6 of 11 Kings of Leon heroes did, median gap 8 days
131 of 136 (96.3%) campaigns record zero return moments
The cross-roster test exists in code and has never been run. The 7–14 day boundary is our convention, not YouTube's.

We publish fewer live sessions and fewer lyric videos than the market does.

Channel-level adoption across 138 channels — whether a channel has ever published the format, not how well it performed. The live gap is the largest we've found between our roster and the comparison set.

ObservedAsk YouTube

Shorts are baseline, not an edge — and we cannot see whether they feed long-form.

Over half of campaigns already use them. Top-quartile campaigns use them more. But we can count Shorts and we can count long-form; we cannot observe one viewer moving between them. We've made that a hard rule internally rather than implying a funnel.

55.1% of 136 campaigns include at least one Short
Top quartile 82.4% vs bottom quartile 34.3%
CHVRCHES · 38 Shorts = 0.4% of visible views
The Snuts · 44 Shorts, median 1,088, 39 comments total
Developing-tier adoption is 32.3% — the real gap is at the small end.
ObservedHypothesis

Reach and capture have come apart on the campaign we know best.

The Snuts' album lands on 23 October. Reach is expanding fast; every durable follower base is flat. If this shape is common, "grew the audience" and "grew the fanbase" are different claims and we've been using them interchangeably.

Monthly listeners +56.2% since 1 Jan

ObservedAsk YouTube

More channels on our roster are declining week-on-week than growing — and we can't tell if that's structural.

One week of full coverage, so treat it as a flag rather than a finding. But it's the question we most want YouTube's view on: is per-video performance falling across the platform as supply rises, and what should a label do about it?

Only 4 of 30 positive on subscribers

86 managed channels, week 25. Single-week, cross-sectional. Cyclical vs structural is untested.

New direction · for discussion, not a result

Turning the Watcher into our own behavioural dataset.

We monitor enough channels now that the interesting question has changed. It isn't "how is this artist doing" — it's "what do campaigns that look like this one normally do?"

To answer that we'd need to tag every channel, then compare campaigns within a bucket rather than across the whole roster.

"Artists should post more on YouTube."
"For UK indie artists this size, campaigns with a second meaningful asset inside two weeks tend to hold viewing longer than campaigns without one."

We are nowhere near the second statement. This is the direction, and we'd rather have it pulled apart now than after we've built it.

Dimensions we'd tag on
Artist size Upload frequency Shorts / long-form mix Campaign behaviour Genre Career stage Catalogue vs frontline Release type Territory

Solid = we already hold it. Dashed = we don't. Genre currently covers 16% of the roster and sits in the wrong layer; career stage and catalogue-vs-frontline don't exist at all.

The honest position

Our one segmentation that works today is a four-state health classification — growing, weak conversion, underfed, cold. Everything richer than that is an intention.

Questions for YouTube

Where YouTube has data, context or product
knowledge we don't.
Segmentation

If you were building our artist segmentation, what would you bucket on?

And more usefully — which of our dimensions would you throw out? We'd rather hear that genre is a red herring now than in six months.

Metrics

Which metrics should we stop using because they mislead?

We've already banned a few internally. We'd like to know which ones you see people misread most often.

Shorts

What do you actually see between Shorts and long-form music consumption?

We've made it a rule not to claim a funnel we can't observe. But you can observe it — is there a relationship worth designing around?

Follow-up

Does structured post-release follow-up affect recommendation longevity?

We keep finding the gap. We can't test whether closing it does anything, because reverse causation is wide open — teams add follow-ups to releases that are already working.

Benchmarks

Are there benchmarks you could share by artist size, genre or format?

Even coarse ranges would stop us building our own from 140 channels and calling it a market view.

Supply

Are average views per video falling as consumption grows — and what should a label do about it?

More of our roster is declining week-on-week than growing, while cadence holds. We can't separate cyclical from structural from outside.

Collabs

How should we evaluate a collaboration when we don't own both sides of the analytics?

HEARTSTEEL is the live example. The release sits on a game publisher's channel with four featured artists attached.

Demand vs supply

What signal separates real audience demand from simply publishing more?

This is the one that would change how we advise. Right now we can tell a busy channel from a quiet one, not a wanted release from a loud one.

Products

Stations and CTV — what's real, and who's eligible?

Two artists this quarter had a case for a programmed channel. We stopped short of recommending it because we don't know the eligibility position.
