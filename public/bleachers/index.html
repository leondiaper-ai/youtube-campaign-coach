Fifteen music videos have earned 60.2M views between them — 54% of everything we can measure on this channel. That is a catalogue doing its job: a small number of properly made things carrying the weight, rather than volume spread thin.

The publishing has been anything but steady, and that is worth seeing plainly. 2024 was the busiest year on record at 57 uploads. 2025 carried ten. 2026 has already put out fifteen, including a 91-minute concert film. The channel moves in bursts around the work.

One number to hold on to. The 230 uploads we can read sum to 111.0M views against a lifetime counter of 189.0M. We cannot account for the difference from public data, so every share on this page is calculated against the 111.0M we can actually inspect — not against the channel total. Where a figure looks smaller than you expect, that is usually why.

Read this carefully, because it cuts both ways. The two songs with the full set are also the two oldest and the two biggest, and a hit attracts assets as much as assets build a hit. Age and scale are tangled up in this, and nothing here proves the build caused the size.

What it does show cleanly is that the channel used to build song ecosystems and now mostly doesn't. Alfie's Song in 2018 was the last release to get a lyric video and an official audio. Every song since has had a video and, at best, one live performance — which is a decision worth making on purpose rather than by default.

The Stone Pony show is 91 minutes sitting as one upload. It has done 227,036 views as a full set, which is a good number for a feature-length concert film — and it is also a room full of individual song performances that have never been published as individual song performances.

Cutting that set into per-song videos is the clearest opportunity on this channel: the footage exists, the rights are presumably settled since the full show is already public, and the format it would create is the one the Dirty Wedding Dress comparison suggests this audience turns up for.

This is a steer, not a verdict. Two Shorts out of ninety-five is a thin basis for a rule, and the honest reading is that it points the same direction as the live finding rather than proving anything on its own.

But the pattern is consistent: when this channel shows the band playing, people watch. When it announces that something is coming, they mostly don't — the release-announcement Shorts around you and forever ran between 8,756 and 46,996 views against a music video that did 2.2M.
Where we would start

Three things,
in order.

Do first — the footage already exists
01

Cut the Stone Pony set into song videos

Ninety-one minutes are already public as one upload at 227,036 views. Published as individual songs, that same footage becomes the format the channel's own numbers favour — the Electric Lady take of Dirty Wedding Dress outperformed its music video by 1.9×, and the best-performing Short on the channel is a live performance clip. Nothing new needs shooting.

Then — a cheap test on the next single
02

Give one upcoming song the full build

Video, lyric video, official audio and a live take, on one release, and watch what the package does against the video-only releases that came before it. The two songs that ever got this treatment are the channel's two biggest — that is suggestive rather than proven, and one controlled release is what would turn it into something you can trust.

03

Point the Shorts at the performances

The two Shorts that cleared 50,000 views were both performance footage; the announcement Shorts sit near the 6,054 median. A Shorts layer cut from live material, pointing back at the full performances, costs nothing extra once the Stone Pony set is broken up.

What this read cannot see. Everything here comes from public data — views, likes, comments, durations and dates. Without Studio access there is no watch time, no retention, no traffic source and no audience breakdown, so nothing above is a claim about how long anybody watched or where they came from. The recommendations are framed as tests for that reason.
