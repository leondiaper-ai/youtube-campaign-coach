YouTube: observations and ideas for the OKR discussion

Six months of exploring how to make YouTube easier to see, easier to talk about and easier to act on — and where that might help the group.

Where this starts

A shared objective, measured together.

Zack’s intention

Agree a YouTube objective we’re all bought into. Choose quarterly results we can measure. Review how we did, and learn together.

What I’m bringing

Over the last six months I’ve been exploring ways to make YouTube more visible across teams, turn channel information into useful artist conversations, and find where support could help.

Here’s what’s been useful, and where it might help us shape and measure OKRs.

Better visibilityUseful artist ideasKnowing where to focusUnderstanding what changes
Better visibility

Once people can see the channels, joining in gets easier.

What it helps us do

See key channel information across the roster — who’s active, who’s gone quiet, how a release week compares with the weeks before — with or without Studio access. That gives more colleagues the context to engage, contribute ideas and share what they’re learning.

An example

When a team asked whether YouTube could extend the GENER8ION × Yung Lean STORM moment after the VMAs, the shared data showed the growth had started two weeks earlier and was led by Asia. That helped inform the recommendation on what to try first.

Could we build on this?

UK, Nordics and Australia work from the same view today. More teams looking at the same picture would make YouTube a shared conversation rather than a specialist one.

Useful artist ideas

A channel reading becomes something the artist team can act on.

What it helps us do

Understand what already works on a channel, then suggest next steps that fit the artist — drawing on YouTube’s own best practice rather than inventing a formula.

Amyl and The Sniffers

Live is already one of their strongest formats: a typical live video does around seven times a typical Short. And none of the last album’s four videos had a long-form follow-up inside two weeks. So the ideas are packaging performances already filmed, and planning each release’s follow-up before the hero lands — not more filming.

Could we build on this?

The same reading has been done for Partisan artists (Ezra Collective, IDLES) and for Defected’s label channel, where it mostly showed what the channel already does well. Any priority artist or label could have one.

“Superb work on this, it is genuinely impressive. I love how you integrated our best practices into a pitch for the artist.” YouTube UK · feedback on the deep-dive approach

Could we build on this?

Four things we could track together.

Wider engagement

More teams working from shared YouTube information, and bringing it into their own conversations.

More consistent activity

Priority artists active in more weeks, with shorter gaps between uploads.

More follow-up

Priority releases followed by another music moment, rather than one video and silence.

More views and consumption

Release weeks against the weeks before, and territory consumption over comparable periods.

Targets and ownership are for the group to decide. Three questions to start:

Three lenses, read separately and never combined into a score: what our pipe reports for the UK, what Chartmetric estimates for UK audience, and the global channel figures. D-Block was the one artist all three agreed on, which is what made it the first to get a closer look.

Rankings are scoped to the dataset reviewed. “#2” is within the 145 artist strings in our UK consumption data for the period, and “#1” is within the 30-artist sample given Chartmetric territory reads. Neither is a claim about UK rap or about Virgin’s roster as a whole: the Virgin UK universe reviewed has only three rap artists, so a “#1 Virgin artist” line was removed from the D-Block deck for exactly that reason.
What the consumption data is and isn’t. VMG UK’s own YouTube reporting (Daily Trend, YouTube tab) for repertoire through our pipe: 51.0M views across 145 artist strings in the period. It is period-level, carries no Shorts/long-form, art-track or UGC split, and is not every UK YouTube view Virgin generates. About half the consumption strings are not yet matched to a tracked channel, and some Virgin UK artists with real UK audiences (Peter Gabriel, Bloc Party, Ezra Collective, IDLES among them) have no record in it at all — which is why the slide says local knowledge has to sit beside the data.
Status. The D-Block review (/dblock) was built for the management conversation. Its recommendations are options; none is recorded as implemented, and no result is claimed. The 474M gap between the channel’s lifetime counter and the sum of its public uploads is unresolved and no share-of-lifetime claim depends on it.

The four measures — what could be read today

Wider engagement
Readable now: teams with live boards and artists they have added; reviews requested and delivered. Not a proxy: a workspace existing.
More consistent activity
Readable now from public upload dates: active weeks and longest gap per campaign window, for any tracked channel.
More follow-up
Readable now: whether a priority release is followed by a long-form moment within an agreed window. The window itself is a choice for the group, not a rule.
More views and consumption
Readable now: release-week channel views against the preceding weeks (Watcher weekly snapshots, from late August 2026); UK consumption per artist from our own reporting, period-level. With authorised Studio access in aggregate: traffic sources, returning viewers and the catalogue-to-new-release question, which public data cannot answer.
