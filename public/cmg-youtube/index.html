Create Music Group × YouTube · exploratory review
VMG UK · Exploratory review · 14 October 2026

Create Music Group
on YouTube

Findings from Create’s YouTube channel ecosystem: what we observed, where channels differ, what the examples suggest, and what remains unknown.

Commissioned to understand owned channels better. This is research to inform discussion, not a strategy recommendation. Public data only: 8,687 uploads from 19 channels, Oct 2023 – Oct 2026.

Findings in brief

A few channels show a narrow, consistent view distribution. The largest numbers come from individual assets and culture formats.

Consistent distribution

  • Trap Nation, Chill Nation and Monstercat Uncaged: 0–0.5% of full tracks under 10K views at 3–18 months old
  • On Trap Nation’s latest upload, 10 of 27 sampled comments discuss its new intro
  • Monstercat’s Shorts link back to its own releases (44 of 68 on Uncaged)

Individual assets

  • Chill Nation slowed/sped edits: median 276K vs 34K for originals, in a small sample
  • Trap Nation’s largest uploads coincided with 2023–24 trends
  • Benchmark 7clouds: lyric videos of established hits, median 405K

Clips and games

  • House Nation (3.9M subscribers): typical full track 5.6K; Rap Nation tracks 3.1K
  • Their largest results are clips and games: one Rap Nation clip 88M
  • High clip views coexist with much lower full-track views; transfer between them wasn’t measured

Concentrated lifetime views

  • Every public upload on 23 channels: 39,215 uploads, 32.1B lifetime views
  • Trap Nation holds 65% of the Nations’ lifetime views; the top 1% of Nations uploads hold 64%
  • One upload carries 59% of phonk and 66% of Monstercat Instinct

Older uploads still earn

  • Trap Nation gained 467M views from Feb 2025 to today; at least 98% went to uploads published before then
  • Same pattern on Monstercat (91–97%), Nettwerk (96%), House and Bass Nation
  • Exceptions: Rap Nation and Bass Boosted, where new uploads carry most of the gain

Featured artists

  • The strongest execution is on artists’ own channels: bbno$ (daily own-sound Shorts, 2.1M typical full track), DeeBaby (2.0M)
  • Recent network placements are modest: median 7K for bbno$ and 3.7K for DeeBaby (last 24 months)
  • Marshmello’s 753M network total sits 91% on 2016 uploads

What remains unknown: whether viewers return, where views come from, and what any of this earns. Modest views relative to subscribers do not establish poor economics, limited incremental value or an inactive audience.

Four shapes in the network

  • A wide catalogue baseTrap Nation’s median upload has 630.8K lifetime views; its largest holds just 3.8%. Monstercat Uncaged (median 402.2K) and House Nation look similar.
  • One or two uploads carry the channelphonk: one 2025 slowed edit holds 59% of lifetime views. Monstercat Instinct: “Crab Rave” holds 66%. Strut: 54% on one upload.
  • Clips alongside musicFlighthouse’s views are 95% Shorts (games and trends). Rap Nation: 41% of lifetime views sit on non-music clip Shorts, 59% on tracks.
  • Older, smaller cataloguesLatin, R&B and Indie Nation, Lowly and nettwerkbackstage have not uploaded since 2022–24; their views sit on that older catalogue.

Cumulative is not compounding. A lifetime total says how many views an upload has gathered since it was published, not whether it is still gaining. Older placements show far larger totals than recent ones (Marshmello: median 940K for uploads over five years old, about 50K for those under two), but they have had years longer to accumulate, so the two are not comparable.
Persistence is measured only briefly. We re-read all 39,215 uploads 17–31 minutes after the first read (14 Oct 2026, about 18:30 UK). In that short window, 18 of Marshmello’s 58 placements registered new views, led by 2016 Trap Nation uploads (“Alone”, “Keep it Mello”). “Alone” also gained on Monstercat Uncaged, so two copies of one song were drawing separate, observable listening at the same time. Across Trap Nation, 92% of the window’s new views went to uploads more than three years old. This is one evening snapshot. Whether placements keep gaining over weeks is unresolved; re-reads of the same uploads at +24 hours and +7 days have been prepared.

We don’t describe network views as incremental: they may replace listening that would otherwise have happened on the artist’s own channel or elsewhere.

Artist channel, network layer, and what public data can’t separate

For these artists, the visible results sit mainly on their own channels. The network adds more uploads, with smaller visible results.

The artist channel carried the largest results in every case: bbno$ 2.1M and DeeBaby 2.0M typical full tracks. The clearest systematic execution was bbno$’s: own-sound Shorts every day, each linked to the full track.
The network layer adds more copies, versions and remixes. Recent placements are modest: medians of 3.7K (DeeBaby), 7K (bbno$) and about 50K (Marshmello) for the last 24 months. Large network totals belong to 2014–18 uploads.
Not attributable from public data: what each deal includes, any paid or label marketing, traffic between channels, and whether network views add listening or move it. Four of the twelve (Sevdaliza, Omarion, Durand Bernarr, Lauren Sanderson) have one Nations placement or none.

What this review helps us understand

Different operating approaches are visible in the network, and they show different public results.

Channel purposes differ

Listening destinations, label catalogues and culture formats each show a distinct view pattern.

Two measures, two stories

Subscriber scale and per-upload views measure different things. Neither alone describes a channel’s value.

Large results sit near existing demand

The biggest uploads coincided with trends, known songs or versions. An association, not a cause.

Repertoire moves inside the group

Monstercat releases reappear on Trap Nation within weeks; broke.’s own channel drew more than the Nations copies of its releases.

Features follow channel type

Label channels link Shorts to releases; curators organise playlists by occasion.

The limits are real

Returning viewers, traffic sources, catalogue earnings and economics are not visible publicly.

Questions for further discussion

Questions these findings raise.

  1. What would returning-viewer or traffic-source data clarify?Whether a consistent view distribution reflects returning viewers, subscriber feeds or recommendation.
  2. Would the listening / culture distinction matter when defining a channel’s purpose?The two formats show very different per-upload results, sometimes on the same channel.
  3. Could different treatments be assessed separately?Originals, remixes, slowed edits, translations and lyric videos behave differently in our small samples.
  4. How do label and network channels share a release?Monstercat and broke. show different patterns of where a release draws its visible views.

Areas that may warrant further exploration

Repeated snapshots over several weeks · authorised analytics from a partner channel · release case studies chosen in advance · measures of value beyond visible views (playlists, Content ID, DSP follow-through).

Analysis appendix

Public YouTube data collected 14 October 2026 (13:26 and 15:42 UTC, plus page-level checks to 18:00 UTC). An exploratory review, not a strategy recommendation.

What this review does and does not claim

It describes what is publicly visible: uploads, lifetime view counts, publishing dates, Shorts links, playlists and a sample of comments. It does not show returning viewers, subscriber vs non-subscriber views, traffic sources, watch time, revenue, cost or paid support. Modest views relative to subscribers do not establish poor economics, limited incremental value or an inactive audience. Network views are not assumed to be incremental: they may displace listening that would otherwise happen elsewhere.

The objection to raise first. A narrow, consistent view distribution can come from subscribers returning or from steady recommendation. Public data cannot tell these apart; both are compatible with what we see.

Definitions

Short
Listed on the channel’s Shorts tab. A ≤62-second duration proxy agreed almost exactly.
Typical full track
Median lifetime views of non-Short uploads aged 3–18 months (90–540 days) at the snapshot, so channels are compared at similar ages.
Repost
Same core title, a shared credited artist and the same version tag, with the network upload 0–60 days after the label’s. Each was checked by hand.
Established song
The same song also has a 7clouds lyric video in the data. This is a proxy for demand that exists elsewhere.
Growth window
New views between two readings 2h16m apart on one weekday afternoon (UK). Exploratory only, and never extrapolated.
Small samples. Chill Nation slowed/sped edits n=11; !K7 n=7; Strut n=4. Medians on these are descriptive only.
Lifetime views are not first-week results. Older uploads have had longer to accumulate; comparisons are made within an age band for that reason.

Current consumption (exploratory)

All 39,215 uploads were re-read once, 17–31 minutes after the inventory read (re-read 17:26–17:38 UTC, 14 Oct 2026). YouTube refreshes public counts in batches, so only 12% of uploads registered any change, and 869 showed small decreases (count corrections, set to zero). Results by group, as shares of the window’s new views: Nations 45% on uploads over three years old and 33% on uploads under a year old; electronic labels 81% and 13%; culture channels 1% and 97% (99% Shorts). Nothing here is scaled to a day, month or year. Re-reads at +24 hours and +7 days are prepared to measure persistence; until then it is unresolved.
